Color isn't
decoration.

Most designers pick colors because they look nice together. That's decoration. New in 2.0 · How color actually works

Your color picker
lies to you.

Every color is three decisions: hue (which color), lightness (how bright it reads), and chroma (how colorful it is). The problem: the HSL sliders every tool shows you measure none of these the way your eye does. Two colors with identical HSL lightness can differ wildly in how bright they look. This is why palettes that compute fine still look wrong.

The trap

HSL is lightness-blind

HSL yellow at 50% lightness glows; HSL blue at 50% lightness is nearly dark. Mute a mid-lightness amber by dropping HSL saturation and you don't get "quieter amber" — you get khaki sludge. Darken it for contrast without re-saturating and you get olive-grey. If your accents keep coming out muddy, this is why.

The fix

Think perceptually: OKLCH

OKLCH measures lightness and chroma the way people actually perceive them, and it's now native CSS supported in every modern browser. Equal steps in OKLCH lightness look equal — across every hue. Build your ramps and tokens there, ship hex codes out the other side.

Proof

The same ramp, two ways

Ten steps of one blue, evenly stepped. The HSL row bunches: the middle steps blur together and the ends jump. The perceptual row spaces evenly — every step is a usable UI state.

Perceptually stepped: every neighbor pair is the same visual distance apart.

One more thing

Screens got wider than your hex codes

Modern displays show Display-P3 — roughly 25–30% more colors than sRGB, mostly in the saturated greens, cyans and magentas. Hex codes can't reach them. You don't need P3 to build a great brand, but know it exists: the electric accent you see on your MacBook may quietly dull on the office monitor, the projector, and the printed page. Test the places your brand will actually live.

New in 2.0 · The world map of color

Meanings
don't travel.

Color psychology is mostly learned, not hardwired — shaped by religion, history, and environment. A 64-country research review confirmed it: some associations hold broadly, but red, white, and green swing hard by region. McDonald's runs saturated red as a background in India, where red is auspicious — and reduces the same red to an accent in Sweden. Same brand, same color, different deployment. That's the lesson.

The deployment rule · Where most palettes go wrong

60 - 30 - 10.
The rule.

Every great brand palette is three colors deployed in roughly these proportions. The math isn't exact — the principle is. Equal-weight colors fight. Stacked-weight colors sing. This is why every brand you respect feels effortless: it isn't. It's deployed.

60%

The Dominant

The base color the eye spends most time on. Usually the lightest or most neutral of your three. It's the canvas, not the brand. Backgrounds, large surfaces, breathing room.

EXAMPLES Cream, off-white, deep black, soft grey.

30%

The Brand

The color people will remember as "your brand color." Logo, primary buttons, hero blocks, anchor moments. Strong but not overwhelming.

EXAMPLES Forest green, navy, hot magenta, kiwi green.

10%

The Accent

The pop. The thing that catches the eye. Highlights, CTAs, illustration moments, micro-interactions. Used sparingly so it stays special.

EXAMPLES Golden yellow, electric lime, coral, carrot orange.

Two bright colors at 50/50 is a fight. One dominant, one supporting, one accent is a brand. Pick which color is doing which job before you pick the colors.

The laws · The seven things most designers get wrong

7 laws
of color.

These aren't style preferences — they're structural rules separating "student work" from "brand identity." Every palette in this Bible follows them. Most failed palettes break at least one.

01

Pick a dark anchor before you pick a bright

Bright + bright = chaos. Bright + dark = identity. Every loud brand that works has a black, navy, or charcoal grounding the noise.

02

Saturate the brand color, mute the accent

If both colors are at full saturation, they fight. If both are muted, the brand looks tired. One leads at full power, the other supports at 60–80%.

03

Test it at 5% of the screen, not 50%

Most combos look great as a giant swatch and terrible as a real interface. Build a fake landing page mock with realistic proportions before locking the palette.

04

Always design the dark mode at the same time as the light

If your brand color only works on white, you don't have a brand color — you have a website background. Test every accent against both black and white.

05

One bold color is a brand. Three bold colors is a circus

If you need a third loud color, the first two aren't doing their job. Add neutrals, not more brights.

06

Use color for hierarchy, not decoration

Color should tell the eye where to go. Headline > body > CTA. If you can't name what each color is doing for the user, the palette is decoration, not design.

07

Borrow a palette from a category you're NOT in

Beauty-brand colors for a SaaS, hospitality colors for fintech, sportswear colors for healthtech. Standing out is mostly refusing to look like your category.

New in 2.0 · What this decade forced

The 2026
amendments.

The seven laws stand. But brands now live in more places than the laws anticipated — ten interface states, two color modes, forty countries, a hundred screen types. Three amendments cover the gap.

08

Build the ramp, not the swatch

A brand color isn't one hex anymore — it's a family. Hover states, pressed states, tinted surfaces, borders, disabled states, charts. Build a ten-step tint-to-shade ramp for your Brand and Accent the day you pick them, stepped perceptually so every neighbor pair looks evenly spaced. If the color only works at one step, it's a poster, not a system.

09

Contrast is a floor, not a vibe

Body text needs 4.5:1 against its background (WCAG AA); large text and UI elements need 3:1. That's the legal-grade floor — meet it in BOTH your light and dark deployments. And know where the standard is heading: newer perceptual models (APCA, the candidate for WCAG 3) score dark-on-light and light-on-dark differently and demand more contrast from thin, small type. Translation: test with your real font at real sizes, in both modes, not with a giant bold swatch.

10

Check the culture map before crossing borders

Your palette ships worldwide by default now — app stores don't have borders. Before entering a market, check what your Brand color means there (page 04). You don't have to change the color. McDonald's didn't. You change the deployment: what's a 30% hero in Mumbai may need to be a 10% accent in Stockholm.

The seven laws make a palette work. The three amendments make it survive contact with the world.

New in 2.0 · Accessibility is deployment

If they can't
read it, it isn't branding.

Roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of color-vision deficiency. Add sunlight on a phone screen, cheap projectors, and aging eyes, and a meaningful slice of every audience sees a different palette than the one you shipped. Accessibility isn't a compliance chore bolted on at the end — it's Law 03 and Law 04 taken seriously.

RatioWhat it clearsUse it for
4.5 : 1WCAG AA, normal textBody copy, labels, anything under ~18pt. Your non-negotiable floor.
3 : 1WCAG AA, large text & UIHeadlines 18pt+, buttons, icons, input borders, focus states.
7 : 1WCAG AAALong-form reading, small dense UI, anything for older audiences. Aim here for Ink-on-Dominant.
Rule of thumb 1

Never color-only

If a state is communicated only by hue — red vs green status dots, colored-but-unstyled links — 8% of your male users can't see it. Pair every color signal with a shape, icon, label, or weight change.

Rule of thumb 2

Both polarities, real text

Dark-on-light and light-on-dark with the same ratio don't read equally — perception is polarity-aware, and thin small type needs more contrast than the math suggests. Test your actual font, weights, and sizes in both modes. A ratio calculated on a bold swatch proves nothing about your 14px regular body.

Where the standard is going

WCAG 2.2 today, perceptual tomorrow

WCAG 2.2 AA is the compliance target now and for the next few years. WCAG 3 is coming with a perceptual contrast model (APCA is the leading candidate — polarity-aware, font-size-aware, not yet final). The two-layer strategy: WCAG 2 ratios as your floor, perceptual thinking as your ceiling. Nothing in this Bible requires you to choose — a palette that respects Laws 01, 04, and 09 clears both.

New in 2.0 · From palette to system

A palette is a poster.
A system ships.

The gap between "we picked colors" and "our product looks coherent" is systemization. Four moves take you across it.

Move 1

Name colors by job, not by hue

Tokens named brand, accent, dominant, ink survive a rebrand; tokens named teal-500 don't. The 60-30-10 roles ARE your token architecture — one variable per job, light and dark values behind each.

Move 2

Ship both deployments as one artifact

Law 04 operationalized: your CSS custom properties carry the light values, a prefers-color-scheme block carries the dark. One file, two moods, zero "we'll do dark mode later."

Move 3

Ramp every working color

Amendment 08: Brand and Accent each get a ten-step tint-to-shade ramp, perceptually stepped. Tints become surfaces and hovers; shades become borders and pressed states. The ramp is where a palette becomes an interface.

Move 4

Respect the print gamut

Process inks can't reproduce every screen color — saturated limes, cyans, and violets dull the most on press. Carry CMYK values from day one, flag the risky swatches, and proof against a calibrated profile before packaging. The screen is not the only surface your brand lives on.

Hand a developer four hexes and you've given them homework. Hand them roles, ramps, and both modes and you've given them a brand.
